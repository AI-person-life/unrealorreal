# Where we drew the line

Building synthetic characters is technically easy. The hard question is what you
*don't* do with them.

🇭🇺 [Magyarul](../hu/etika.md)

## We say they're machine-made

Wherever these characters appear, it is stated that they are synthetic and that
their images are machine-generated. This isn't legal caution, it's a question of
genre: the unstated version would be deception, and that's a different activity.

## We don't claim expertise

The characters give no medical, legal or financial advice, and claim no
credentials. Explaining things: yes. Professional advice: no. The line sits where
someone would make a decision about their own situation based on the answer.

## What's publishable from measurements, and what isn't

In wireless surveys, **the ratio is public interest; the address is not.** What we
publish: how many networks, in what proportion, with what settings. What we never
publish: identifiers, network names, coordinates, timestamps.

The measurement is passive — counting publicly broadcast identifiers. No
connection, no intrusion.

## We don't name victims

When a real case is instructive, the case goes out, not the person. No names, no
usernames, no identifying details — not even with consent, because published
writing outlives consent.

Likewise: we don't print the addresses of scam sites. An article that spells one
out hands it traffic and search ranking. The lesson is the method, not the URL.

## We don't run third-party code on readers

The accompanying site carries no ads, no analytics, and no external loads — even
the fonts are embedded. The reason isn't privacy zeal, it's consistency: ad
networks are themselves a malware delivery route. Writing about defence while
running someone else's code on your readers' machines is self-contradiction.
